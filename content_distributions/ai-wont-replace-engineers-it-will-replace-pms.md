**Pain points**:

**Quick example**:
- Old: user call → PM takes notes → requirements doc → sprint → build → ship
- New: user call → engineer understands it live → builds it

## LinkedIn

**Story**: Two years of "AI will replace engineers" articles. All wrong target.

**Problem**: The conversation is focused on the wrong role. Engineers are not the ones at risk from AI coding agents. The translation layer is.

**WTF moment**: When code takes an afternoon instead of weeks, sitting on a call with a user and shipping before it ends becomes possible. At that point, a PM in the middle is just friction. I've done this. The user describes the problem, I understand it technically in real time, and we're building before the call is over.

**Solution**: Engineers who move up into the product layer — who own the full loop from user problem to shipped solution — become more valuable. The non-technical PM role loses its reason to exist.

**Draft post**:

---
Everyone is worried AI will replace engineers. I think they're looking at the wrong role.

Here's what project managers actually do: they translate user pain into engineer requirements. That's the job. Everything else — roadmaps, sprint planning, requirements docs — is scaffolding around that translation.

That role exists because engineers historically didn't want to talk to users. And writing code was expensive, so you needed someone to carefully pre-process what got built.

Code is now cheap. The workaround doesn't hold.

When I can sit on a call with a user, understand their problem technically in real time, and ship a fix the same afternoon — what is a PM doing in the middle of that loop? Just friction.

The engineers who come out ahead are the ones who can own both sides: technical judgment *and* direct user understanding. They become one-person product teams. Not because they're superhuman, but because the cost structure changed.

The engineers who don't adapt — the ones who are purely there for the code — get squeezed. Not by AI directly, but by product-aware engineers who cost the same and do more.

And the non-technical PM? The one without real technical grounding? That role is evaporating now.

The one archetype that genuinely wins: the deeply technical PM, or the engineer who moved into product. They understand both sides natively. No translation needed.

In my opinion, this isn't about replacement by AI. It's a role consolidation. One where engineers who move up win, and where the translation layer in between loses its reason to exist.
